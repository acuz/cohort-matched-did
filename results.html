Constant effect

Every treated unit has the same effect, \(ATT(g,t) = 2\). Any gap between an estimate and the flat line is bias from the comparison group, not from effect heterogeneity.

With a constant effect, TWFE and pooled CS coincide with CM-CS when parallel trends hold unconditionally. As soon as the trend depends on \(X\) or \(Z\), comparing a cohort with the controls matched to other cohorts, which have different covariates, biases both. CM-CS stays on target whenever the trend is driven by the matching covariates, whatever its functional form.

Heterogeneous effects add a second problem for TWFE: some of its implicit comparisons use already-treated cohorts as controls, so it is biased even with no trend at all. Pooled CS avoids these comparisons but keeps the pooling bias.

Covariate-dependent effect

The effect depends on \(X\) with cohort-specific slopes, so each cohort’s average effect depends on its covariate composition: \(ATT(4) = 2\), \(ATT(7) = 3.125\), \(ATT(10) = 5\).

What CM-CS does not fix

CM-CS removes the pooling bias, but it is only as good as the matching itself:

  • Latent trend drivers (Latent U, Step in latent U). Matching on \(X\) and \(Z\) balances \(U\) only insofar as \(X\) and \(Z\) predict it, which leaves a small bias. All estimators are affected, CM-CS the least.
  • Covariates that move after matching (Drifting X). Units that look alike at \(g-3\) drift apart afterwards. Matching on the covariate trajectory rather than on a single snapshot largely removes this bias: see the conditioning page.
  • Cohort-specific slopes unrelated to \(X\) (Cohort-specific slopes). Here the matching covariates carry no information about the trend.

Cuzzola & Tiedtke (2026), Not All Scars Are Equal: Job Loss Along the Wage Distribution, working paper.

 

Simulation outputs: S = 100 replications (bias), S = 300 (coverage); N = 1,950 units (750 treated, 1,200 never treated).
